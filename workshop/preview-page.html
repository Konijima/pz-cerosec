CeroSec - Unix Network Terminal

Made by Konijima · Project Zomboid · Build 42

The desktop computers of Knox County still work. This one runs Unix.

CeroSec puts a small 1993 Unix on the vanilla desktop computers already standing in the world. Right-click one to switch it on, right-click again to sit down at it, and a green 60x20 terminal opens on a BIOS line and a login prompt. Behind it: files, permissions, accounts, a shell with pipes and cron, the doors and lights of the building under /dev, and three ways off the machine. The disk belongs to the computer, not to you, so a session you walk away from is still running when you come back and a machine you carry across the county keeps what was on it.

No dependencies. Build 42. Safe to add to a save you are already playing.

  • A real terminal. Sixty columns by twenty, a BIOS that counts the memory and names what it found, a login prompt, a shell. Two survivors at one computer read and type on the same glass.
  • Files, permissions, accounts. Owner, group and other, three octal digits, symbolic modes, symbolic links. Accounts, groups, su and sudo with a real /etc/sudoers behind it.
  • The prompt is the script language. sh's own grammar: if, while, for, variables, $( ), pipes, redirects, background jobs, jobs and kill. cron runs a crontab on the minute hand while nobody is watching. edit turns the screen into an editor.
  • The building, under /dev. A character device for every door, switch, lock and window the machine can reach, worked by name: dev light0 off, dev door1 open, dev lock1 toggle. A vanilla Motion Sensor in the room becomes a sensorN a script can poll.
  • Hardware you have to fit. Nothing is a device because of what it is. A magnetic contact, a relay, an electric strike or a door operator goes on the fixture itself, with a screwdriver and a level of Electricity, and only then is that door on /dev. Find the boxes in an electrician's van, or read the CeroSec Field Wiring Guide and build them.
  • Three ways out of the case. Coax to the other machines on the premises, a modem to the county at 2400 baud, and a two-way radio that outlives both. Details below.
  • Floppy disks. A 3.5 inch disk with a filesystem of its own: newfs, mount, copy your notes onto it, write on the label, carry it across town.
  • A manual you have to find. Three volumes, printed in 1993, spawning where a book about a computer would have been shelved. They open as a book on your screen and your survivor goes on doing whatever they were doing.

The first ten minutes, in order:

  • Right-click a desktop computer, Turn on computer. It needs power in the room.
  • Right-click it again, Use computer. Your survivor walks over and sits down.
  • At login: try admin and press Enter at the password. A bare machine has admin and root, both open. A machine that belonged to somebody has his accounts instead, and some are locked.
  • help lists the commands. dev lists the doors, lights and locks this machine can reach.
  • That list is empty until somebody has screwed a module onto a fixture. Right-click the door, the window or the light switch itself and take CeroSec hardware.
  • Type exit, or close the window, and walk away. The screen keeps running and is exactly as you left it next time.

Knox County had computers in it before the outbreak, and people were using them. A machine nobody has switched on yet comes up as somebody's machine.

  • Ten kinds of premises, ten kinds of machine. A house, an office, a dispatch room, a bank, a shop, a school, a clinic, a radio station, a military post, and CeroSec Systems' own bench. Each with its own hostname, accounts, files, and a week of /var/log from the last days anybody came to work. Their crontab lines still run: a shop's lights go off at nine, a bank's vault pulls itself to at six.
  • The password is written down, because everybody wrote it down. A yellow sticky note in a desk of that same premises. A dead employee with his own login folded in his pocket. The name of the item in your inventory is the password.
  • And the paper is right before you get there. The note and the machine both derive the password from one number made once for the life of the save. Find the note on Monday, switch the computer on a week later, and it wants exactly what is in your pocket. The next save answers differently.
  • Disks with something on them. Seventeen floppies in a hundred are not blank: utilities, a BBS list, games, somebody's backup of his home directory on the 8th of July, a distribution disk. Each has a README.TXT in DOS capitals saying what the rest of it is.
  • The phone book is a phone book. Vanilla's own Phonebook item, which until now only relieved boredom, is the Knox County directory. Look up numbers opens the yellow pages of one exchange, and the copy remembers which town it was picked up in.

All of it is one sandbox option, Prefilled machines and disks, on by default. Turn it off and every computer is bare. Either way, a machine somebody has already switched on is never touched.

  • Coax, for the premises. Every computer of one premises shares a segment, with an address of the form 10.b.b.n that comes out of where it stands. A premises is not a building: a house is one, a mall is thirty shops on thirty segments. arp, ping, ruptime, rlogin, rsh, rcp, and /etc/hosts.equiv and .rhosts to stop typing the password.
  • The telephone, for the county. One line per premises, one call at a time, a seven-digit number the firmware announces at power-on and nowhere else. cu dials at 2400 baud. It is a party line, the way a rural exchange really sold one.
  • Packet radio, for after the power. A two-way radio the machine can reach becomes its TNC on the serial port, and a callsign in /etc/callsign makes it a station. cu -l /dev/radio0 opens the box's own prompt, with the TNC-2's command set: MYCALL, CONNECT, DISCONNE, CONV, MHEARD. Anybody tuned to that frequency hears the connect. It is the only link that outlives the county's power.

Every disk lives on the server and every command runs there. A client asks; it never decides, and no client is ever told a password. Two survivors at one machine share the screen, a third can rlogin in from next door, and what one writes the others read.

The budgets are why it is safe on a public server. A script is stepped, never looped to the end, so a while true somebody left running costs that machine its own share and nothing else. A disk is 64 KB, a file 4096 bytes, a directory 96 entries, a machine 512 nodes; four jobs is four jobs and the fifth remote session is refused. Nothing a player types is ever handed to Lua, in either direction.

Admins get a debug window behind the game's own debug mode: every machine the server holds, the selected one's filesystem, its /dev, the wire, the scheduler and the log. It also hands out a diagnostics floppy that runs twenty-six checks of the shell on the machine in front of you.

Where a real /bin/sh, a real cron, a real rlogin or a real Hayes modem would answer a certain way, this machine answers the same way, refusals included. The commands wear the names 1993 gave them: useradd, userdel, usermod -G, and not Debian's adduser.

Six things have no 1993 model and are kept anyway. Rather than pass them off quietly they are named in the engine and printed on a page of the in-game Volume 1 called What is not Unix here: help, dev, mkpasswd, edit, sudo, and jobs that belong to the machine rather than to the shell, because this shell can be walked away from.

Does it need any other mod? No. No dependencies, no bundled libraries, vanilla Lua only.

Can I add it to a save I am already playing? Yes. The computers were always there; this gives them a disk the first time you switch one on. Removing it leaves them as furniture, and the manuals, floppies and modules in inventories are lost.

Will it slow the game down? It is budgeted rather than trusted. A script runs a fixed number of steps and then waits its turn; a machine nobody is looking at costs one pass on the minute hand. The ceilings above are the whole of what a computer can cost.

Does it work in multiplayer? Yes, and it was built that way rather than adapted. Dedicated-server testing is the next thing on the list, so if you run one, the comments are the right place for what you find.

Will an update break the machines I built? No. Every release photographs the exact shape it writes to the save, and the next one has to walk that photograph forward and prove the accounts still log in, the files are byte for byte and the disks still mount, or it does not ship.

Does it conflict with other mods? Nothing known. It adds its own items and menu entries and overrides no vanilla file. A mod that replaces the desktop computer object itself is the one thing that would collide.

Build 41? No, and there will not be one. This is Build 42 only, on 42.20 and up.

I cannot get into a machine. last names the accounts that have been used, and the passwords are a plain word and two digits. Failing that, the 1993 answer is the one the firmware gives: hold the switch through the BIOS and let it repair the system, which reinstalls it and keeps /home.
